# Build FlatHub as one ledger, four views

Build FlatHub as a small TypeScript monorepo (Expo for the flat app, Supabase Postgres with row-level security per household) around a few **append-only ledgers**: purchases, which write both stock lots and shared expenses; stock moves; chore completions; and meal logs. Then make the four screens (ration, fridge, money, duties) thin views over them. Nothing published does this. Commercial apps are siloed: Splitwise and Tricount handle money, Sweepy and Tody handle chores, NoWaste and KitchenPal track a single user's fridge. Flatastic comes closest to all-in-one, but it has no fridge ownership and no South Asian payment rails. The Bangladeshi "mess manager" apps that do model meal rates and bazar duty get poor reviews for their UI and their paywalled books. Open source has strong single-purpose engines (Grocy for inventory, Spliit and SplitPro for splitting, Donetick for chores, KitchenOwl for shared lists), but every GitHub repo tagged "flatmates" or "roommates" is a toy with six stars or fewer. No repo links the cost of a ration purchase to the split. Hands-on tests in this research found real fairness bugs in the reference code. Spliit's settle-up ignores amounts. Grocy and Donetick both count a *skipped* chore as a *done* one. The common mess-accounting formula fails to balance unless the manager's cash pot is included. Each has a small fix. The verification pass also corrected several web-sourced figures in the earlier notes, and this report uses the corrected values throughout. The retail POS can share the product catalogue, units of measure, purchase documents, the stock-lot ledger, FEFO picking and a single integer `Money` type. It should not share members, splits, tax or tenders.

## No product combines ration, fridge, money and chores for a South Asian flat

The commercial market splits into four camps that ignore each other.

**Expense splitters.** Splitwise dominates expense splitting in India, but it now limits free users to **"up to 4 expenses each day"**, a number it publishes in its own help centre ([Splitwise KB](https://kb.splitwise.com/pro/what-is-splitwise-pro)). The earlier notes said the cap was unpublished and "about 3–5". Verification showed that was wrong. When the paywall arrived, users accused Splitwise of "paywalling many of the previously free features without any notice" ([X post linking Reddit, Dec 2023](https://x.com/ArtemR/status/1740150704268849568?lang=en)). The App Store lists Pro purchases at $4.99 and $39.99 (₹49 to ₹1,399 in India) but does not label which price is monthly and which is yearly ([App Store US](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/splitwise/id458023433); [App Store IN](https://apps.apple.com/in/app/splitwise/id458023433)). Splitwise has no UPI integration, so settling means switching to a payment app and typing the amount by hand ([Niptao, competitor blog](https://niptao.app/en/blog/splitwise-alternative-india-free)). Tricount went the other way after the bank bunq bought it: it is now free, but it dropped CSV/PDF export and statistics rather than unlocking them ([tricount Help Center](https://help.tricount.com/articles/what-happened-with-tricount-premium)).

**All-in-one flat apps.** Flatastic, the German flat-share ("WG") app, bundles chore rotation with points, a shopping list, expenses and a bulletin board ([Flatastic](https://www.flatastic-app.com/en/)). It costs **$1.99/month or $18.49/year** for one person in the US store, or $6.49/month for a whole-household tier ([App Store US](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id840810742)). The earlier notes' "$2.49/month" figure was refuted.

**Chore and fridge apps.** Sweepy puts multi-user households behind a $2.49/month premium tier ([mwm.ai](https://mwm.ai/apps/sweepy-home-cleaning-schedule/1498897320)). Users complain that its effort points "do not reflect real task difficulty" ([appshunter](https://appshunter.io/ios/app/1498897320)). OurHome, once a popular family chore app, has disappeared: both store listings now return 404 ([ourhomeapp.com](http://ourhomeapp.com/)). Fridge apps are single-user tools, and researchers found **no app that tracks whose food is whose** in a shared fridge ([cohabby](https://cohabby.com/blog/best-apps-for-roommates/); [Servanda](https://servanda.ai/resources/roommate-ate-my-food-fridge-rules)).

**Bangladeshi mess apps.** The most relevant prior art comes from Bangladesh, where bachelor messes use a standard accounting model. Meal rate = total bazar (grocery spending) ÷ total meals. Each member pays their meal count times that rate. Fixed costs such as rent, Wi-Fi and the bua (maid) are split equally. A "stop meal" has to be requested before a cutoff ([Mess Meal Rate Calculator BD](https://studentmealcalculator.convertertoolsai.com/)). Mess Manager has **100K+ installs and a 4.39 rating**, but reviewers call it "very poor UI" and object that past months and PDFs are paywalled per member, so only paying members see the full books ([Play Store](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.m27lab.messmanager.app&hl=en)). MessBuddy adds bazar rotation with one-tap swaps and three rate algorithms. It is free for up to 8 members, and premium costs ৳14.49 per member per month ([MessBuddy](https://messbuddy.org/)). The lesson is that the accounting model is validated but no app has executed it well.

**Research.** Studies of shared living point to what the app should optimise. *Perceived* fairness, not actual hours worked, predicts satisfaction ([Koster et al. 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0192513X21993899)). A CHI 2025 study found that household coordination tools "even unintentionally increase the burden" on whoever already carries it. Participants also held back chore complaints for fear of seeming to nag ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3706598.3713383)). A sociologist interviewed by MIT Technology Review put it plainly: "People get defensive when notified of inequality" ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2022/05/10/1051954/chore-apps/)).

The conflict data is concrete. Over two thirds of people polled have fallen out with a housemate over cleaning ([SpareRoom](https://www.spareroom.co.uk/content/info-flatsharing/avoiding-arguments/)). Where incomes differ, energy use (77%), bill contributions (52%) and communal supplies (48%) top the list of tensions ([SpareRoom blog](https://blog.spareroom.co.uk/how-wage-gaps-can-spark-friction/)). These are consumption problems, and an equal split makes them worse. That is why the meal-rate model, and weighted splits such as "the AC room pays more", belong at the core of the design.

## Open-source household apps converge on lots, minor units and rotation enums

The mature open-source projects agree on a handful of data-model decisions, even though their stacks differ.

Star counts come from the GitHub API on 2026-10-02 and were confirmed in the verification pass.

**Inventory (Grocy).** Grocy is the best model for monthly rations. Every purchase becomes its own **stock lot** with a best-before date, price and location. Every change goes into an append-only, undoable `stock_log`. Each product carries separate units for purchase, stock and consumption, with product-specific conversion factors that override global ones ([StockService.php](https://github.com/grocy/grocy/blob/HEAD/services/StockService.php); [migrations](https://github.com/grocy/grocy/tree/HEAD/migrations)). A 25 kg rice bag bought on the 1st therefore becomes one lot in kilograms, which is drawn down by the cup. Products below their `min_stock_amount` are added to the shopping list automatically, which is the closest existing thing to a monthly ration list.

Grocy's weaknesses are just as instructive. It has no household or tenant column anywhere. Its maintainer says splitting it into homes "would mean kind of rewriting and rethinking everything" ([issue #644](https://github.com/grocy/grocy/issues/644)). Much of its logic sits in about 50 SQL views. Receipt OCR has been requested since 2019 ([issue #404](https://github.com/grocy/grocy/issues/404)), and users call the app "overkill" ([Lemmy](https://lemmy.world/post/11033097)).

**Expense splitting (Spliit and SplitPro).** Both store money as **integers**: Spliit uses minor units (paise) and SplitPro uses BigInt. Spliit apportions each expense with the largest-remainder (Hamilton) method, rotating ties by a hash of the expense id ([shares.ts](https://github.com/spliit-app/spliit/blob/HEAD/src/lib/shares.ts)). Its receipt scanning sends the photo to an OpenAI vision model with a strict JSON schema; it does not use Tesseract ([receipt action](https://github.com/spliit-app/spliit/blob/HEAD/src/app/groups/%5BgroupId%5D/expenses/create-from-receipt-button-actions.ts)). SplitPro runs recurring bills as `pg_cron` jobs ([scheduleService.ts](https://github.com/oss-apps/split-pro/blob/HEAD/src/server/api/services/scheduleService.ts)). IHateMoney and KitchenOwl still store money as floats, which is the anti-pattern to avoid.

**Chores (Donetick).** Donetick models a household as a "circle". Its assignment-strategy enum (round_robin, least_assigned, least_completed, random, random_except_last_assigned, keep_last_assigned, no_assignee) is a ready-made list of chore strategies. It also separates rolling schedules, which count from the completion date, from fixed ones ([model.go](https://github.com/donetick/donetick/blob/HEAD/internal/chore/model/model.go); [scheduler.go](https://github.com/donetick/donetick/blob/HEAD/internal/chore/scheduler.go)).

**Shopping (KitchenOwl).** KitchenOwl adds cheap intelligence worth copying for a ration list. A 1-D DBSCAN over checked-off timestamps detects shopping trips. A topological sort over those trips learns the order of the store's aisles. Apriori rules suggest "you usually also buy…" ([jobs/](https://github.com/TomBursch/kitchenowl/tree/HEAD/backend/app/jobs)).

**What users keep asking for.** Issue trackers across these projects repeat the same requests: recurring expenses, real user accounts instead of link-shared groups, offline mode, receipt OCR, a pantry inside the shopping app, and working iOS notifications. Examples include Spliit's #5 and #76, SplitPro's #105, KitchenOwl's #10 and Donetick's #269 ([Spliit issues](https://github.com/spliit-app/spliit/issues?q=sort%3Areactions-%2B1-desc); [SplitPro #105](https://github.com/oss-apps/split-pro/issues/105); [KitchenOwl #10](https://github.com/TomBursch/kitchenowl/issues/10); [Donetick #269](https://github.com/donetick/donetick/issues/269)).

**The gap.** None of these projects links the cost of a ration lot to the split. Grocy knows prices but not debts. The splitters know debts but not stock.

**Licences.** The licence column matters if any code goes into the POS. Copying from the MIT projects (Grocy, Spliit, SplitPro, Yuvomi) is safe. Donetick and KitchenOwl are AGPL, which brings source-disclosure obligations if their code ends up in a networked product. Tandoor adds a Commons Clause that forbids selling it, including as paid hosting ([LICENSE.md](https://github.com/TandoorRecipes/recipes/blob/develop/LICENSE.md)). For these projects, re-implement the ideas rather than copying code.

## Hands-on tests caught fairness bugs in the reference code

The verification pass did more than read code. It ported Spliit's split and settlement logic and ran it on a four-flatmate month. It also ran Grocy's real FEFO and chore-statistics SQL views against a rebuilt Grocy schema, and computed a mess month by hand. Donetick could not be executed because a Docker image pull timed out, so its findings come from tracing the code only. All four checks turned up problems that someone copying "the standard approach" would inherit.

**Spliit's settle-up ignores amounts.** The earlier notes said Spliit settles largest creditor against largest debtor. It does not. Spliit sorts creditors before debtors and then by **participant id**, and never compares amounts ([balances.ts L79–126](https://github.com/spliit-app/spliit/blob/HEAD/src/lib/balances.ts)). The code says this is deliberate, so that suggestions stay stable after one person pays. In 20,000 random trials with small whole-rupee balances, Spliit's ordering suggested more transfers than necessary **22% of the time**, against **4.6%** for a true largest-first greedy. The smallest failing case is balances of ₹[−3, 3, 1, −1]: Spliit suggests 3 transfers where 2 suffice. With realistic paise-level balances neither method was ever non-minimal, because coincidental zero-sum subgroups almost never occur. Flats use round numbers ("everyone owes ₹500 for Wi-Fi"), though, so the weakness bites. Only SplitPro's `minCashFlow` actually is largest-first ([simplify.ts](https://github.com/oss-apps/split-pro/blob/HEAD/src/lib/simplify.ts)).

Finding the true minimum is **strongly NP-hard** by reduction from 3-Partition ([Verhoeff 2004](https://wstomv.win.tue.nl/publications/INFE023.pdf)). That is a stronger result than the Subset-Sum argument the earlier notes cited. For a flat it does not matter, because a bitmask dynamic program over subsets is exact and instant for 12 or fewer people. The fix is to try the exact solver first, fall back to largest-first, and use participant id only to break ties so that suggestions stay stable.

**The test month itself balanced.** On a realistic month (₹48,000 rent, electricity weighted toward the AC room, a ₹7,350 ration split by days present, personal items and a reimbursement), every expense's shares summed exactly to its amount, and the net balances summed to zero paise. A personal purchase entered as an expense shared only with yourself netted out correctly, so the ledger needs no special "personal" code path.

**Both chore engines reward skipping.** Grocy's "who did it least" strategy counts every log row that has not been undone, and that **includes skips**. In a SQL run against the real view, a flatmate who skipped twice ranked level with one who actually did the chore twice ([ChoresService.php](https://github.com/grocy/grocy/blob/HEAD/services/ChoresService.php)). Donetick's `least_completed` has the same flaw by construction: `SkipChore` writes a history row with `CompletedBy` set to the skipper, and the strategy does not filter by status. Donetick also breaks ties using Go's randomised map iteration order, so "whose turn is next" is not predictable ([handler.go L4002–4140](https://github.com/donetick/donetick/blob/HEAD/internal/chore/handler.go); [repository.go](https://github.com/donetick/donetick/blob/HEAD/internal/chore/repo/repository.go)).

Rotation after a swap also differs between the two. Grocy's alphabetical rotation advances from whoever *last did* the chore, so if Divya covers Bilal's turn, Bilal's turn is lost. Donetick's round-robin advances from the *assigned* person, which handles swaps correctly.

For FlatHub, treat a **skip as its own outcome that earns no credit**, and record a swap as a transfer of one instance. Assign each chore to the available member with the largest effort deficit (fair share of points minus points done), and break ties deterministically: whoever least recently did this chore, then user id. Pure round-robin is just the special case where every chore is worth the same points and everyone is home.

**The mess formula only balances if the manager's pot is counted.** The commonly stated balance, `deposits + bazar paid − meal cost`, does not net to zero unless the manager also owes the unspent deposit pot. In the sample month the books were off by exactly **₹2,885**, which was that pot. The rounding matters too. A ₹12,291.25 bazar over 230 meals gives a rate of ₹53.4402 per meal:

| Rounding method | Total collected vs bazar |
|---|---|
| Quote the rate rounded to ₹53.44 | 5 paise short |
| Round each bill to the nearest rupee | 75 paise over |
| Largest-remainder apportionment by meal count | exact |

So store the rate as a fraction and round only when apportioning. The model is also worth the effort for fairness: a flatmate away for 10 days paid **₹988.64 less** than under an equal split. That is exactly the perceived-fairness lever the research highlights.

**FEFO needs more than an expiry sort.** FEFO means "first expired, first out". Grocy's real order is: lots at the default consume location first, then opened before unopened, then earliest best-before, then oldest purchase. As a result an opened milk carton is used before an unopened one that expires two days sooner. A lot with a NULL date sorts ahead of every dated lot, which is why Grocy stores "never expires" as 2999-12-31 ([StockService.php](https://github.com/grocy/grocy/blob/HEAD/services/StockService.php)). FlatHub should order lots by `(location = default_consume) DESC, opened DESC, expiry ASC NULLS LAST, received_at ASC`, and warn when an unopened lot will expire first.

**Product pitfalls.** The research names three non-code failures. **Charging for core logging** is the first, and Splitwise's cap is the cautionary tale. **A single admin** is the second: one person setting up and running the app recreates the very mental load the CHI study describes. **Setup friction** is the third, since practitioners report that roommates will not adopt an app that takes about 10 minutes to set up ([Plastnofy](https://plastnofy.com/articles/the-best-chore-apps-for-roommates-in-2026)).

Push fatigue is also real, although the figures come from marketing surveys with unclear methods. One reports that 64% of users would delete an app after five or more notifications a week ([Business of Apps](https://www.businessofapps.com/marketplace/push-notifications/research/push-notifications-statistics/)).

## Expo, Supabase and LLM extraction fit the 2026 ecosystem

**Choosing the client stack.** The two features that most need native access are barcode scanning and push reminders, and both are second-class on iOS web apps. iOS web push works only after the user installs the web app to the Home Screen ([WebKit blog](https://webkit.org/blog/13878/web-push-for-web-apps-on-ios-and-ipados/)), and Donetick's users hit exactly this limit ([#269](https://github.com/donetick/donetick/issues/269)). Safari still ships BarcodeDetector disabled by default ([MDN BCD](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/main/api/BarcodeDetector.json)). Expo avoids both problems: `expo-camera` uses Google's code scanner on Android and DataScannerViewController on iOS ([Expo Camera](https://docs.expo.dev/versions/latest/sdk/camera/)).

There is a fair case for the other option. If every flatmate uses Android, which is plausible in India or Bangladesh, a Next.js PWA works: Android Chrome has had BarcodeDetector since version 83, and Android PWAs receive push. A PWA would also share more code with a Next.js POS. **Pick Expo if even one flatmate uses an iPhone.** Otherwise a PWA-first build with a WASM barcode fallback is acceptable.

**Backend and cost.** Supabase's free tier is ample for one flat: a 500 MB database, 50,000 monthly active users and 200 realtime connections. Free projects **pause after a week of inactivity**, which matters for an app used in bursts, so plan on a keep-alive cron job or the $25 Pro plan ([supabase.com/pricing](https://supabase.com/pricing)). Put `household_id` on every table, add a row-level-security policy keyed on household membership, and invite flatmates through a signed join link shared on WhatsApp.

Spliit's open requests for real accounts show why link-only groups fail for a flat that lasts years ([#76](https://github.com/spliit-app/spliit/issues/76)). Supabase Realtime is enough for live updates, such as the shopping list while someone is in the bazar. Because the tables are append-only ledgers keyed by client-generated UUIDs, offline merging is easy if it is needed later. PowerSync can add offline SQLite sync on top of Supabase, and its free tier covers 2 GB of synced data a month ([PowerSync pricing](https://powersync.com/pricing)).

**Getting data in.** Data entry is where adoption is won or lost. OpenFoodFacts lists only **24,639 Indian and 1,334 Bangladeshi products** and allows 15 product reads a minute per IP ([OFF API](https://openfoodfacts.github.io/openfoodfacts-server/api/); [OFF search](https://world.openfoodfacts.org/api/v2/search?countries_tags_en=india&page_size=1&fields=code)). Much of what a flat buys is loose, unbarcoded rice, dal and atta from a kirana shop anyway. So barcodes should be a secondary path that seeds a household product table, with OpenFoodFacts lookups cached locally.

The primary path should copy Spliit's approach: one strict JSON schema for items, quantities, units, prices, payer and a shared/personal flag, filled by an LLM. The same schema can take typed text ("bought 5kg rice 400"), voice transcribed on the device, or a photo of a printed receipt or handwritten bazar slip, and the result is always shown on a confirm card before anything is saved. Accuracy on Bangla, Hindi and Banglish input, and on handwritten slips, was not measured in this research. Test it before relying on it.

**Settling up in India (UPI).** Settlement can be one tap with a payer-initiated `upi://pay?pa=…&pn=…&am=…&cu=INR&tn=…` link. In NPCI's linking specification the amount is optional for a static person-to-person link, and the payer can edit it ([NPCI linking spec, mirror](https://www.labnol.org/files/linking.pdf)). So build "pay Asha ₹X" buttons that the payer pushes, not "request money" flows. Test the links with GPay, PhonePe and Paytm.

**Settling up in Bangladesh (bKash).** bKash publishes only merchant APIs, with no person-to-person deep link ([bKash Developer Portal](https://developer.bka.sh/docs)). Show the payee's number with a copy button next to the amount instead.

**Reminders.** WhatsApp, where flatmates already talk, costs **₹0.115 per utility message** in India. The first 1,000 service messages a month are free, and utility templates sent inside an open customer-service window are free too ([Meta pricing](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/whatsapp/pricing)). About 30 reminders a month comes to roughly ₹3.45 per flat before tax.

**Monthly ration list.** This needs no forecasting machinery. Log purchases precisely and consumption coarsely ("finished", "half left"). Then target each staple at average monthly use plus a buffer, minus what is on hand, rounded up to pack sizes. This is an inference from standard inventory practice. The notes found no household-specific source on forecasting.

**Design.** The research did not cover visual design. There is no evidence here on typography, colour or motion, and making FlatHub look good is a separate design task. The UX evidence does point clearly at how the app should feel to use. Entry should cost almost nothing: adding an expense is one tap that defaults to the last payer and last split, and meal logging is "on by default, tap to skip" with a daily cutoff, which makes it the daily habit loop. Bangla and Hindi text should be there from the start.

Fairness should be visible without becoming a scoreboard. Every member sees the full books for free, the opposite of the paywalled-history complaint about mess apps. Each member's contributions show against their fair share, with a way to say thanks but no ranked leaderboard. The app sends the reminders so no flatmate has to nag, the admin role rotates or disappears, and the household gets one digest instead of a stream of pings. In the fridge, items are tagged mine, shared or up-for-grabs, a one-tap "used yours, owe you" becomes an expense, and items auto-release to "free for all" after a set number of days. This copies the low-tech "one shelf each plus written rules" convention that flatmates already use ([Marks Out Of Tenancy](https://www.marksoutoftenancy.com/renters-rights/what-to-do-if-housemate-stealing-food)), and no app currently offers it.

## The POS and the flat share a stock ledger, not a business model

Underneath, a household and a shop run the same inventory engine. A bazar run and a shop's goods receipt are both a **purchase document with line items**. A flatmate consuming rice and a POS sale are both a **negative stock move**; the sale just also writes revenue and payment rows. FEFO picking, reorder points and consumption or sales velocity are the same algorithm in both. The barcode pipeline is also the same: ML Kit or expo-camera scanning EAN-13 on the device, plus an OpenFoodFacts cache under the ODbL licence ([ML Kit](https://developers.google.com/ml-kit/vision/barcode-scanning); [OFF API](https://openfoodfacts.github.io/openfoodfacts-server/api/)).

**Shared package.** That justifies one `packages/domain`, with a small test for each part. It holds a `Money` type in integer minor units with largest-remainder allocation, which FlatHub uses for expense splits and the POS uses to spread discounts and tax across lines. It holds units of measure, with global conversions (kg to g) and per-product ones (one packet = 70 g), and the FEFO lot ordering. The shared tables are `product`, `product_uom`, `purchase`, `purchase_line`, `stock_lot` and `stock_move`, each scoped by an `org_id` that is either a household or a shop.

**Kept separate.** On top of that, keep household concepts (members, split rules, settlements, meal logs, chores) apart from POS concepts (GST or VAT, price lists, payment methods, receipts, cash-drawer sessions). Don't invent a generic "party" abstraction until both apps really need one.

**What is unverified.** This section is a design recommendation, not a sourced finding. The notes fetched no POS schemas (such as Odoo's or ERPNext's stock ledgers) and no GST or VAT receipt requirements, so validate the tax side separately.

## Conclusion

FlatHub's distinctive feature is the link no existing product makes: **a purchase is both a stock lot and a shared expense.** A 25 kg rice bag lands in the pantry and in the ledger at the same moment. It is then drawn down by FEFO and, optionally, charged by meal count. Every other feature (the fridge, the ration list, settle-up, the duty roster) becomes a view over the same append-only ledgers. That same purchase-to-stock spine is what the POS needs, so building it once pays twice.

The hands-on work also changed what "copy the reference implementation" should mean. The well-starred code gets money arithmetic right but gets fairness subtly wrong: settlements that ignore amounts, skips that count as work, and ties broken at random. For flatmates, a visible and explainable sense of fairness is the whole product. So the place to spend engineering care is not the stack, which is a solved choice, but a few dozen lines of settlement, rotation and month-close logic. Each of those needs a test that fails if a skip ever earns credit or the books ever stop summing to zero.
